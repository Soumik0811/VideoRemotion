# ODDLY LOGICAL — ANIMATION ENGINE V6
## Reference-Inspired Cartoon Storytelling Upgrade

You are the animation director and senior Remotion engineer for Oddly Logical.

Your task is to upgrade the existing animation engine to produce short educational cartoons with the storytelling rhythm and visual mechanisms observed in the reference video:

**"Are You Carrying Buckets or Building a Pipeline?" — The Curious Plot**

Do not recreate the reference's characters, exact artwork, branding or complete scene compositions. Extract its animation principles and implement them using Oddly Logical's established visual identity.

The uploaded reference is approximately 76 seconds long, vertical 1080 × 1920 at 30 FPS.

The reference uses:
- Simple recurring cartoon characters
- Bold colored radial-burst backgrounds
- A reusable physical story environment
- Animated objects and numerical counters
- Short explanatory concept cards
- Multiplication of objects
- Day/night changes
- Gradually assembled visual mechanisms
- Financial calculations revealed step by step
- Split-screen comparisons
- High-contrast captions
- Quick visual transitions
- Minimal complex body animation

Our goal is to reproduce the **quality of visual storytelling and animation pacing**, not copy its specific artwork.

## PHASE 2 — THE NEW ANIMATION PHILOSOPHY

The reference succeeds because the environment and objects tell the story.

Do not attempt realistic human animation.

Characters should primarily:
- Stand
- Walk short distances
- Blink
- Nod
- Look toward objects
- Change expressions
- Point using simple arm rotation
- Celebrate
- React to surprising outcomes

Avoid:
- Detailed hand articulation
- Realistic grabbing
- Multi-joint inverse kinematics
- Hand-to-hand transactions
- Complex limb choreography
- Unnatural arm stretching

When a character buys or sells something, use a floating object animation rather than a physical handoff.

Example:

Two characters face one another.

A money bill appears above the counter.

The bill moves toward the seller.

A product appears and slides toward the buyer.

The two characters react.

The audience understands the transaction without needing realistic hand movement.

This is our permanent character-animation rule.

## PHASE 3 — SIX REUSABLE SCENE TEMPLATES

Implement six compositional templates that can be automatically selected based on the narration.

### TEMPLATE A — STORY WORLD

Shows a physical environment with relevant characters and objects.

Examples:
- Coffee shop
- Bank
- Factory
- Village
- Airport
- Supermarket
- Office

Requirements:
- Reuse the established scenery
- Add relevant animated foreground and background elements
- Place characters naturally in the location
- Allow camera pans and object-focused zooms
- Use small pop-up labels when needed

The environment should be recognizable.

Do not use an empty generic background for physical story scenes.

### TEMPLATE B — CONCEPT REVEAL

A short, bold explanation screen using a radial-burst background.

Example:

Blue radial background.

An illustrated character appears.

A jar of coins materializes.

Coins fall into the jar.

Large heading:

ACTIVE INCOME

Short explanation:

"Money earned while you work."

Keep this template short and animated.

Use it when introducing an important concept, not for every narration sentence.

### TEMPLATE C — OBJECT MECHANISM

Used to demonstrate how a process works.

Examples:
- A pipeline assembles segment by segment
- Gift-card value decreases after purchases
- Products travel from factory to retailer
- Cash moves between businesses
- A loan balance decreases with payments
- Inventory grows in a warehouse

Objects must physically move, appear, multiply, disappear or transform.

Prefer process animation over text-based explanations.

### TEMPLATE D — MATH REVEAL

Displays important calculations.

Reveal the formula progressively.

For example:

$20 GIFT CARD

Then:

$14 REDEEMED

Then:

$20 − $14 = $6 LEFT

The final number should receive a satisfying pop, scale or highlight animation.

Never display a complicated equation all at once.

Use animated numerical counting only when it clarifies the change.

### TEMPLATE E — COMPARISON SCREEN

Shows two situations side by side.

Examples:
- Active income versus passive income
- Revenue versus profit
- Two business strategies
- Before versus after
- Debt versus equity

Use clear color coding.

Keep both sides visually balanced.

Allow comparison bars, objects and character expressions to change independently.

### TEMPLATE F — FINAL PAYOFF

Reserve the most visually impactful animation for the main conclusion.

Use:
- A large final number
- Animated highlights
- Relevant character reactions
- A short conceptual label
- A brief camera push-in
- Minimal competing text

After the payoff, return to the story environment or a branded ending when appropriate.

## PHASE 4 — RADIAL BACKGROUND SYSTEM

Implement a reusable RadialBurstBackground component.

Support:
- Teal/blue for hooks and explanations
- Gold/green for success and positive outcomes
- Red for losses and negative consequences
- Purple for psychology or behavioral concepts
- Brown/amber for trade-offs and costs

Use Oddly Logical's own brand colors and styling.

Animate the burst very subtly through a slow scale, rotation or lightness variation.

The effect should feel energetic without distracting from the content.

Support optional decorative particles, but keep them minimal.

Do not use the radial background for every shot.

Story-world scenes should retain their actual topic-specific scenery.

Create a reusable AmbientActivitySystem.

Every story environment can define appropriate background animation tracks.

Coffee shop:

- Coffee machine operates

Factory:
- Conveyor belt moves
- Products emerge

- Boxes stack

- Clock moves
- Queue changes
- Monitor updates
- Papers slide across a desk

Village:

- Characters pass
- Clouds drift

Ambient activity should operate independently of the main action.

However, activity must match the setting.

Aim for two or three subtle background animations during a typical story-world shot.

Do not make every background object move at once.

Use deterministic frame-driven motion.

Avoid loops that visibly restart at scene boundaries.

## PHASE 7 — NUMBER AND CONCEPT BOARDS

The reference uses large, bold, readable text.

Create a consistent Oddly Logical typography system.

Major headings:
- Heavy rounded sans-serif
- White or gold
- Dark outline or shadow
- High contrast
- Short phrases

Examples:
- SAME START
- DIFFERENT RESULTS
- $222.4 MILLION
- WHAT CHANGED?
- THE HIDDEN COST
- GIFT-CARD BREAKAGE

Use only one major heading at a time.

Concept cards should contain:
- Small category label
- Main concept name
- One short explanation if needed

Avoid paragraph-heavy boards.

Numbers must be visually larger than supporting text.

For calculations:
- Introduce values sequentially
- Use coherent color meaning
- Emphasize the final result
- Allow sufficient reading time

Do not use complicated dashboard UI when a simple number, equation, or physical object can explain the concept.

## PHASE 8 — STORY PACING

The reference has frequent visual changes without constantly changing physical locations.

Apply the following targets:

- Initial hook within 0–3 seconds
- New meaningful visual event roughly every 1.5–3 seconds
- A larger change of composition approximately every 4–7 seconds when appropriate
- Concept screens typically 2–5 seconds
- Important numerical reveals should be held long enough to read
- Return to the main story environment after abstract explanations

These are editorial guidelines, not mandatory fixed intervals.

Do not add unnecessary transitions just to meet a timing target.

Each visual change should correspond to an action, a number, a consequence, or a new idea from the narration.

One of the reference video's strongest features is its persistent village and river setting.

Apply this principle to all future videos.

For example, a Starbucks gift-card story should reuse:

- One coffee-shop exterior
- One coffee-shop interior
- One checkout counter
- One customer-home environment
- One simple accounting-office environment

Do not regenerate locations when returning to them.

Use the same spatial layout and object designs.

Allow relevant props and environmental states to change while the location remains recognizable.

Keep day/night, time passage, and camera movement available as transitions.
